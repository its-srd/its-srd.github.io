Fan powered suction for inverted vehicle adhesion

Two projects from my MSc in Computational Fluid Dynamics at Cranfield University: my thesis and a group project in deep learning.

MSc thesis, June to August 2025. Supervised by Dr Tom-Robin Teschner.

Objective

The McMurtry Spéirling has demonstrated that fan-driven suction can generate very high downforce which, unlike that of a wing, does not depend on speed. This thesis investigated whether a benchmark road car could remain adhered in an inverted orientation using such a system.

Ceiling Restrictor walls Skirts Twin fans Lower pressure 34.6 kN required 37.9 kN downforce

A modified DrivAer benchmark road car, inverted against a ceiling. The schematic is not to scale: clearances are enlarged for clarity.

Restrictor walls and side skirts, positioned within the wheelbase, seal the underbody to form a suction pocket.

Twin fans beneath the car extract air from the pocket, reducing its pressure. Leakage is confined to the narrow gap under the skirts.

At a suction of 21 kPa the pocket generates about 37.9 kN of downforce, exceeding the 34.6 kN required to support the vehicle and fans with a safety factor of 1.8.

Methodology

  • Prepared the DrivAer reference geometry in SpaceClaim, incorporating underbody restrictor walls, skirts and twin fan openings to form a sealed suction pocket, and produced a watertight model for meshing.
  • Performed steady RANS simulations on HPC with the realizable k-ε model, quantifying the sensitivity of downforce to suction level, ride height and skirt clearance across 17 cases.
  • Automated meshing, solution runs, image generation and file saving with Fluent TUI journals and Python, so that each repeat case required only two or three clicks.
  • Sized the fan system from the CFD results.

Results

The 21 kPa design point produced about 37.9 kN of downforce, 9.6% above the 34.6 kN target. The best-performing configuration had a ride height of about 20 mm and a skirt clearance of about 10 mm.

Increasing either clearance allowed air to leak around the skirts and weakened the suction pocket, showing that effective sealing is critical to inverted adhesion.

Verification and validation

Before comparing design cases, I benchmarked turbulence models, performed mesh independence studies and assessed y+. Hand calculations were used to define and check the targets. The work was presented in a written technical report and a poster.

Deep learning denoising of MRI data

Cranfield University group project, March to May 2025.

Methodology

Trained 3D UNet and diffusion-based models in Python and TensorFlow on large datasets, using the university HPC cluster, and optimised their hyperparameters.

  • Conv 3 × 3, SiLU
  • Copy and concat
  • Max pool 2 × 2
  • Up-conv 2 × 2
  • Conv 1 × 1

A 2D sketch of the architecture, for reference. The encoder receives a noised image at 256 × 256. Convolutions extract features, and each max pooling stage halves the resolution, down to 16 × 16, as the channel count increases from 128 to 512.

The bottleneck holds the most compressed representation of the image.

The decoder restores spatial resolution through up-convolutions, while copy and concatenate connections carry fine detail directly from the corresponding encoder stage.

A final 1 × 1 convolution outputs a single channel: the predicted noise.

Results

  • The 3D UNet achieved a validation SSIM of 0.99, indicating that the denoised images preserved almost all of the structure of their reference images.
  • It achieved a validation PSNR of about 37.3 dB, a measure of residual noise relative to the signal.
